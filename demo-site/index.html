Week 3: Asking a good research question

Synthetic demonstration content written for this project. It is not real course material.

A research question tells your reader what you want to find out and why it matters. A good question is narrow enough to answer with the time and data you have, but open enough that the answer is not obvious before you begin. Many first drafts are either so broad that no single study could answer them, or so narrow that the answer is a single number with no wider meaning.

Start from a topic you care about, then ask who is affected, where, and over what period. Each of these choices narrows the question. For example, “How do students use the library?” becomes “How did first-year students at one university use the library during the examination period in 2025?” The second version tells you whom to ask, where to look and when.

Three checks before you commit

  1. Can you name the data you would need, and can you actually get it?
  2. Would a reasonable person disagree about the answer before you collect evidence?
  3. Does the question avoid assuming its own answer?

When a question fails one of these checks, revise it rather than starting again. Keep a short log of each version and the reason you changed it. Your supervisor will often ask how the question developed, and the log makes that conversation easier.
